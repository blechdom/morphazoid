# Hocket Luigi: sources and framing

Hocket Luigi explores one shared musical result distributed among several performers: **which voice owns each moment, where do voices meet, and what new rhythm appears when the parts are heard together?**

The page labels its material in three ways:

- **Documented relationship** — a specific interlocking rule or pitch cipher described in the cited research is preserved.
- **Reduced structural study** — a documented ensemble principle is compressed to two, three, or four browser voices so it can be inspected and changed.
- **New composition** — a Morphazoid pattern uses ideas such as alternation, phase, cyclic subdivision, or relay without claiming to be a traditional piece.

Editing a sourced study creates **your variation**. It may remain related to its source, but it is no longer presented as the source pattern.

## Bali: kotekan and pola cak

Balinese *kotekan* joins complementary parts, commonly called *polos* and *sangsih*, into a fast composite figuration. Simple *nyog cag* alternates the parts. Other kotekan techniques can include shared or simultaneous tones, so “interlocking” does not always mean exactly one voice per pulse.

Hocket Luigi’s Nyog Cag preset preserves that **documented alternation relationship** while using a newly composed contour. It is not a transcription of a complete gamelan piece.

Source: Wayne Vitale, [“Kotekan: The Technique of Interlocking Parts in Balinese Music”](https://gamelan.org.nz/wp-content/uploads/2015/02/Kotekan-article-Balungan.pdf), *Balungan* 4, no. 2 (1990), American Gamelan Institute.

Kecak’s *pola cak* place several repeating vocal patterns inside a shared frame. In the documented *cak telu* relationship, three parts share one rhythmic-duration sequence at different entry points over four subdivisions of the beat.

The Cak Telu preset preserves the **one-pulse entry relationship** while reducing the source to a legible 16-pulse browser study. It is not a simulation of a Kecak performance. Kecak is a Balinese dramatic and vocal-gamelan genre with a particular twentieth-century history—not a generic “monkey chant.”

Sources: Kendra Stepputat, [*The Kecak and Cultural Tourism on Bali*](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781800103092_A42901913/preview-9781800103092_A42901913.pdf), chapter 1; and [“Performing Kecak: A Balinese Dance Tradition Between Daily Routine and Creative Art”](https://doi.org/10.5921/yeartradmusi.44.0049), *Yearbook for Traditional Music* 44 (2012).

## Buganda, Uganda: amadinda

In amadinda music, two musicians repeat equal-rate pitch sequences. The *okwawula* falls halfway between the pulses of the *okunaga*, creating a composite stream at twice either player’s rate. A third part, *okukoonera*, follows a pattern that emerges from selected pitches in that stream. The ear can also regroup recurring pitches into “inherent” melodic-rhythmic lines that no player performs alone.

The study based on *Olutalo Olwe Nsinsi* preserves a **published 24-event pitch cipher** split between the two interlocking parts. Its digit grid is an analytical re-encoding for this interface, not indigenous notation or a complete account of performance practice.

Source: Gerhard Kubik, [“The Structure of Kiganda Xylophone Music”](https://journal.ru.ac.za/index.php/africanmusic/article/view/606), *African Music* 2, no. 3 (1960), based on Kubik’s practical study with Evaristo Muyinda.

## Central African Republic: Banda-Linda horn hocket

In the Banda-Linda horn repertory documented by Simha Arom, individual musicians contribute fixed pitches and distinct rhythmic figures. Their parts mesh into strict hocket polyphony, and the full ensembles can be much larger than the four voices available here.

The Ongo Relay preset is therefore a **new structural study**: a small, newly composed relay that makes fixed-pitch ownership easy to inspect. It is not a transcription of Banda-Linda repertory.

Sources: Simha Arom, [“Polyrhythmics as a Way to Polyphony: Hocket”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/african-polyphony-and-polyrhythm/polyrhythmics-as-a-way-to-polyphony-hocket/B5695A612A5D11FC8A97DD78D9286081), in *African Polyphony and Polyrhythm*; a [Smithsonian Folkways recording](https://folkways.si.edu/horn-ensemble-ganza-knogo-ngo-banda-linda/music/track/smithsonian) and its [liner notes](https://folkways-media.si.edu/docs/folkways/artwork/UNES08043.pdf); and the [CREM-CNRS archive notes](https://archives.crem-cnrs.fr/archives/collections/CNRSMH_E_1996_013_001/related/5/download/).

## South Indian rhythmic ideas

South Indian classical rhythm offers cyclic *tāla*, spoken *solkattu* and konnakkol, changing subdivisions, and cadential *korvai*. These are rich resources for thinking about a shared time grid, but the sources consulted do not establish a general traditional “Indian hocket” practice.

The Solkattu Relay preset is therefore labeled a **new cross-tradition experiment**: a Morphazoid hocket placed on a four-subdivision counting grid, not a traditional Indian pattern or authentic konnakkol.

Sources: David P. Nelson, [*Solkattu Manual*](https://www.weslpress.org/9780819575234/solkattu-manual/); Eirimas Velička, [“Tāla: Features of Rhythm System in Indian Classical Music”](https://www.journals.vu.lt/acta-orientalia-vilnensia/article/view/18351); and J. Balaji, [“Korvais in Karnatak Music”](https://www.shanlaxjournals.in/journals/index.php/sijash/article/view/8123).

## Tuning and timbre non-claim

All sounds on this page are synthetic timing markers. They do **not** reproduce the tuning, instrument construction, touch, damping, breath, vocal production, ensemble balance, acoustic space, or culturally learned performance practice of the traditions discussed above. Pitch numbers indicate relative positions only. These studies are tools for hearing structural relationships, not claims of authentic sound or performance.

No third-party audio, photography, notation images, or other media are bundled with Hocket Luigi. The interface graphic and browser sounds are original to Morphazoid.
