Names, mechanics, meaning

P1 · P2 · P3 · quasicrystals

P1 is Penrose’s original pentagonal family. P2 is the kite-and-dart system—the names were supplied by John Conway. P3 is the equivalent thick-and-thin rhomb system. This instrument now exposes all three, the Robinson half-tiles, and de Bruijn’s pentagrid construction.

There is no finite “all tilings” list: infinitely many global Penrose arrangements exist, and every legal finite patch recurs. The 64-window control samples distinct pentagrid phases while the presentation control covers the standard geometric views. Seamless mode follows a fixed-size cut through one unbounded plane; finite patch reveals an actual generated boundary.

The rhombs alone can repeat periodically. Their matching marks, or the equivalent Robinson-triangle substitution used here, are what force aperiodicity. Inflation and deflation reproduce the hierarchy at powers of φ; the thick-to-thin count approaches φ too.

The canonical angles are vital: 36°, 72°, 108°, and 144° close the legal vertices and encode the golden ratio. A single tile’s angle cannot change while its neighbors stay fixed. The angle-shear control therefore moves every shared vertex with one affine map, keeping the patch seamless while clearly leaving canonical Penrose geometry.

Penrose later said he had been looking for hierarchical models in which simple roots produce large-scale complexity, with a possible connection to cosmology. He anticipated a new crystallographic possibility, but initially doubted nature could assemble it without non-local foresight. The later quasicrystal diffraction results were therefore both startling and gratifying to him.
